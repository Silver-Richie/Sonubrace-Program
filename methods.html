How Sonubrace measures blood flow

This page documents the whole signal chain, from the ultrasound pulse leaving the transducer to the three parameters on your screen. Every equation here is implemented twice in this repository: in C, in firmware/sonubrace_dsp.c, which is what the microcontroller runs; and in JavaScript, in assets/js/dsp.js, which is what this website runs. The two are kept deliberately identical so a result computed in the browser matches one computed on the device.

Why Doppler, and why continuously

Blood flow changes constantly — with posture, with stress, with the time of day, and over years with lifestyle and disease. Most non-communicable diseases touch haemodynamics somewhere: heart disease acts through the vessels directly, and diabetes acts through blood viscosity, among other routes. A single clinic measurement catches one moment of that.

Doppler ultrasound is well suited to filling the gap because it is non-invasive, needs no consumables, and returns a velocity rather than a proxy. Sonubrace packages it into a wrist-worn band so the measurement can be repeated as often as the user likes, and streams the result to this platform over Bluetooth Low Energy so the trend is visible rather than locked in a device.

Phase 0 — transmit

A microcontroller drives the chain, clocked by a crystal oscillator that fixes the sensor timing. Everything downstream depends on that clock being stable: the Doppler shift is a frequency difference, so clock drift would read as blood movement.

  1. The controller emits an ultrasound pulse.
  2. The pulse is multiplied by one segment of the truncated long code, producing a coded waveform.
  3. The coded waveform is multiplied by the carrier signal.
  4. The DAC converts the result to analogue and hands it to the transmit/receive switch, which passes it to the transducer.

At the transducer the signal undergoes the first of two energy conversions: electrical signal becomes ultrasound. The echo coming back performs the second, ultrasound becomes electrical — and it is that echo which carries the Doppler shift.

The Doppler relation

fD = 2 · f₀ · v · cos θ / c

  • fD — Doppler frequency shift, in hertz
  • f₀ — transmitted ultrasound frequency
  • v — blood velocity
  • θ — the angle between the beam and the vessel
  • c — speed of sound in tissue, taken as 1540 m/s

The factor of 2 is there because the sound makes a round trip: it is Doppler-shifted on the way in and again on the way out.

Truncated long code

There is a genuine tension in pulsed ultrasound. A short pulse gives good resolution but carries little energy, so its echo is weak and noisy. A long pulse carries more energy but smears out in time, destroying resolution.

Coded excitation resolves it. Transmit a long waveform modulated by a known code, then on receive correlate the echo against that same code. The correlation compresses the long echo back into a sharp peak, and the compression gain is roughly the code length — so you get the energy of the long pulse with the resolution of the short one.

Sonubrace takes a further step, following Qin et al. (2012): rather than reusing one code, it generates a single long sequence and truncates it into segments, using a different segment for each transmit burst. Because segments of a maximal-length sequence correlate poorly with one another, the receiver can tell which burst any given echo belongs to. That is what allows bursts to overlap in flight without confusing each other, and it is why the Doppler signal can stay continuous rather than being sampled in isolated snapshots.

Implementation

The code is generated by a 16-bit linear-feedback shift register with taps at x¹⁶ + x¹⁴ + x¹³ + x¹¹ + 1, giving a maximal-length sequence of 65 535 chips. The default configuration takes 16 segments of 32 chips. The quality of a segment is measured by its peak-to-sidelobe ratio — how far the true correlation peak stands above the code's own self-noise — reported by sb_code_psl_db().

Phase 1 — receive

  1. Amplifier. The returning echo is tiny and is amplified first.
  2. Time-gain compensation. Echoes from deeper tissue have travelled further and are more attenuated. TGC applies a depth-dependent gain so a deep vessel is not systematically quieter than a shallow one.
  3. Band-pass filter. Keeps the band around the carrier and discards the rest.
  4. ADC. The echo becomes a digital signal.
  5. Quadrature demodulation. The digitised echo is split to two mixers: one driven by a carrier at phase 0, giving the in-phase channel I; the other at phase π/2, giving the quadrature channel Q.
  6. Decoding. Each channel is matched-filtered against the code segment that was transmitted, restoring resolution and gaining signal-to-noise.
  7. Recombination. The decoded data from the separate truncated segments are combined, and the Doppler signal is ready.

Why two channels rather than one

A single mixer produces a signal proportional to |fD| — the magnitude of the shift, with the sign thrown away. That would leave forward and reverse flow indistinguishable, and the methods this platform implements are explicit that using an absolute value is not acceptable. Because I and Q are a quarter cycle apart, the pair forms a complex signal I + jQ whose spectrum is asymmetric about zero: positive frequencies mean flow toward the transducer and negative frequencies mean flow away. Direction survives, and it survives for free.

One more stage runs before analysis: a wall filter, a high-pass on the slow-time signal. Vessel walls and the probe itself move slowly and reflect strongly, producing a large near-DC clutter component that would otherwise dominate the spectrum. The default cut-off is 60 Hz.

From Doppler signal to spectrogram

The Doppler signal is a time series whose frequency content changes moment to moment — that changing content is the heartbeat. A single Fourier transform over the whole recording would average all of that away, so Sonubrace uses a short-time Fourier transform: slide a window along the signal, transform each windowed slice, and stack the results.

Doppler signal → STFT → P(f, t) → Doppler equation → Doppler spectrogram

P(f, t) is the power present at frequency f at time t. Displayed with time on the horizontal axis, signed Doppler frequency on the vertical and power as brightness, it is the Doppler spectrogram. The default window is 256 samples of Hann with a hop of 64, giving roughly 62.5 Hz of frequency resolution and 4 ms between columns at the default 16 kHz slow-time rate.

The resolution trade-off

A longer window resolves frequency more finely but blurs time, so a sharp systolic upstroke smears. A shorter window sharpens time but coarsens the velocity axis. This is not a tuning oversight; it is the uncertainty principle, and 256 samples is the compromise chosen for cardiac timescales.

Parameter 1 — velocity

At each moment, the strongest frequency present is the one belonging to the fastest blood in the sample volume. Finding it is an argmax over the spectrogram column:

fD(t) = argmaxf P(f, t)

Sonubrace refines that with parabolic interpolation across the peak and its two neighbours, which recovers sub-bin precision and turns a stepped envelope into a smooth one. The Doppler relation is then inverted to give velocity:

v(t) = c · fD(t) / (2 · f₀ · cos θ)

Tracing v(t) across the whole recording gives the velocity envelope. From it: the peak systolic velocity (PSV) is the height of each systolic peak, the end-diastolic velocity (EDV) is the value just before the next cycle begins, and the mean velocity is the time average.

Velocity is the parameter most directly tied to symptoms. Flow that is too fast is associated with hypertension; flow that is too slow can accompany hypotension and is one of the indications discussed alongside hyperglycaemia.

cos θ dominates everything

Every velocity on this platform is divided by cos θ. At θ = 60° an error of 5° changes the result by about 15%. At θ = 80° the same 5° error changes it by more than 50%, and at 90° the cosine is zero and no velocity can be measured at all. This is precisely why the IMU exists, and why the angle guide is not a nicety.

Parameter 2 — flow direction

Direction follows from the sign of the Doppler shift:

  • fD > 0 — flow toward the transducer (forward, antegrade)
  • fD < 0 — flow away from the transducer (reverse, retrograde)

There is a real difficulty here worth naming: systolic and diastolic flow can produce numerically similar shifts with opposite signs. Taking an absolute value would collapse them together and lose the distinction entirely. The truncated-long-code receiver avoids this because the in-phase and quadrature channels already encode the sign — the complex spectrum is simply asymmetric, and the two cases land on opposite sides of the baseline.

Sonubrace reports the direction as the power-weighted balance between the two sides. It also flags brief early-diastolic reversal separately, because in a limb artery that reversal is normal — it is the pulse wave reflecting from the periphery — and its absence is the more interesting finding.

Parameter 3 — flow pattern

Pattern is built from three measurements, none of which suffices alone.

Periodicity — does the waveform repeat?

Rxx(τ) = Σt x(t) · x(t + τ)

Autocorrelation compares the signal with a delayed copy of itself. If the flow is regular, some lag τ makes them line up almost perfectly and Rxx peaks sharply there — that lag is the cardiac cycle length, and the peak height is the periodicity score. Sonubrace normalises it to 0–1 and treats anything below 0.35 as irregular.

Spectral bandwidth — how spread out are the velocities?

BW = fhigh(t) − flow(t)

At a single instant, how wide is the band of frequencies present? A narrow spectrum means the blood cells are moving together at a concentrated velocity. A wide one means many different speeds coexist. This is the measurement that identifies anomalies: disturbed flow past a narrowing spreads velocities out, and on the spectrogram that shows up as the dark window beneath the envelope filling in.

Waveform morphology — what shape is the beat?

Shape is captured through PSV, EDV and mean velocity, which combine into RI and PI below, together with the acceleration time from cycle onset to systolic peak and the consistency of that shape across successive beats.

How the three measurements combine into a pattern class
PatternSignatureWhat it suggests
Laminar High periodicity, narrow spectrum Blood moving together — the expected healthy picture
Pulsatile PI > 4.0 or RI > 0.92 Strong systolic surge, little diastolic flow; stiffer vessels or high downstream resistance
Turbulent Spectral broadening > 0.55 Velocities spread widely; disturbed flow, classically past a narrowing
Irregular Periodicity < 0.35, or beat variation > 120 ms Beats not repeating at a steady interval — a matter for a clinician with an ECG
Damped Acceleration time > 120 ms with RI < 0.70 Slow, blunted upstroke; the downstream signature of a proximal obstruction

Supporting indices

These are computed from the three main parameters, not measured independently. They exist because they are the numbers clinical literature is written in.

Resistive index

RI = (PSV − EDV) / PSV

How much of the flow disappears between beats. RI near 1 means flow almost stops in diastole, indicating high resistance in the vascular bed downstream.

Pulsatility index

PI = (PSV − EDV) / mean velocity

Assesses the elasticity of the vessel and the variability of flow across the cycle. Because it divides by the mean rather than the peak, it stays informative even when diastolic flow reverses and RI saturates.

Alongside these the platform reports the S/D ratio (PSV / EDV), the acceleration time, the heart rate and its beat-to-beat variability, all derived from the same cardiac-cycle segmentation.

The machine-learning layer

Haemodynamic status changes continuously, and it has to be assessed in a form every user can act on. The interpretation layer takes the whole chain as input — the transmitted frequency, the modulated frequency, the Doppler shift, the truncated-long-code processing, the Doppler signal, the STFT output, the spectrogram, and the three parameters — together with the user's health profile.

The shipped analyst is a deterministic feature-based model: a transparent, additive scoring function over those features. That is a design choice, not a placeholder. A screening tool that people are meant to trust should give the same reading for the same recording every time, and should be able to show exactly which factor contributed what — as the analysis page does, line by line. A black box producing an unexplainable number would be worse than no number.

The output has two halves, matching the flowchart's terminal nodes: a spectrogram explanation that makes the picture readable to a general audience, and risk management that names general, non-prescriptive steps to raise with a clinician. Where a supervised model is later trained on a labelled cohort, it slots in behind the same interface — the feature vector it would consume is already assembled by SonubraceAI.contextBrief().

Limitations

Stated plainly, because a screening tool that overstates itself does harm.

  • Angle dependence. Every velocity is scaled by 1/cos θ. An unmeasured or misjudged angle is the largest single source of error in the whole system.
  • Site dependence. The reference ranges built into the analyst are for a peripheral wrist artery. Applying them to a carotid recording would misread it.
  • Single sample volume. The band measures where it sits. A normal reading says nothing about a vessel elsewhere.
  • Aliasing. Doppler shifts beyond half the pulse repetition frequency wrap around. Very high velocities can therefore be under-read.
  • Not validated against a clinical reference. The reference ranges and classification thresholds here are defaults drawn from the general literature. They have not been calibrated against duplex ultrasound in a clinical cohort, and should be before any claim is made about diagnostic performance.
  • Not a diagnostic device. Sonubrace measures haemodynamics and flags patterns. It does not diagnose, and it does not replace clinical assessment.
